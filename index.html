About the Poem

Michèle Lalonde (1937–2021) composed “Speak White” in October 1968, reclaiming an infamous racist insult used to enforce English dominance over French-speaking Canadians. Spoken in a collective voice, the poem reappropriates the slur to expose the linguistic, economic, and cultural subjugation of Francophone Quebec. By drawing explicit parallels to global anti-imperialist and civil rights movements—from Vietnam and the Congo to Watts and Little Rock—Lalonde framed Quebec’s struggle within a broader history of resistance. Her iconic reading at Montréal’s Nuit de la poésie on March 27, 1970, catapulted the work into the cultural canon, and its 1974 publication as a poster-poem cemented its place as a landmark artifact of Quebec’s linguistic politics.

Visualizing the Voices

While Michèle Lalonde’s original text is laid out in a single continuous column, I saw it operating across multiple distinct registers simultaneously. To translate this polyphonic quality into my double-sided poster (presented in French and English), I decomposed the poem into three structural columns:

  • The Left Column holds the marginalized perspective—the voice that confronts and names the unvarnished truths of systemic oppression.
  • The Middle Column carries the voice of institutional authority, delivering state propaganda and the underlying mandate to “speak white.”
  • The Right Column captures the dominant culture’s performative benevolence, which I placed directly adjacent to the left column so each voice forces a reexamination of the other.

In this web experience, I designed these three voices to initially rise as isolated streams, allowing their distinct tensions to build before they finally converge into the unified, single-column architecture of Lalonde’s original text.
